text-to-image (T2I) generative models are extensively used in the art and creative industries because of their remarkable capability in generating high-quality images. The generation of ideal images in a single attempt is nearly impossible, necessitating complex and precise post-image editing. However, stylization pose significant challenges in post-editing. In this context, we introduce the Art Creator, which facilitates style controls based on a simple description or a single image. Art Creator enables nuanced image style edits, alterations in painting materials, colors, and brushstrokes, and understanding of high-level attributes such as object shapes. Furthermore, we manually annotated and released a dataset named ChinArt, comprising over 20,000 eastern artworks, aiming to address the gap in the global art creation domain. The creator, Allen, used Midjourney by inputting different phrases, producing over 900 renderings for his selection. From these, he picked three favorites and further refined them in Photoshop. To enhance resolution, he employed super-resolution reconstruction before printing the final compositions on canvas. In the past two years, diffusion-based large-scale text-to-image generative models have made substantial advances, including Imagen (\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/SahariaCSLWDGLA22}), DALL·E 2 (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2204-06125}), Parti (\cite{DBLP:journals/tmlr/YuXKLBWVKYAHHPLZBW22}) and Stable Diffusion (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/RombachBLEO22}). These models have shown remarkable semantic and compositional generative capabilities, attracting a lot of attention from both the research community and the public, particularly in the art and design industries. However, understanding the learned concepts during their training and influencing their output accurately is an ongoing challenge. Achieving an one-shot text-to-image generation is deemed impractical due to this complexity. Consequently, text-guided image generation is recognized as a highly iterative process. In spite of the formidable generative capacity exhibited by Text-to-Image (T2I) models, their application in the creation of artworks is not without challenges. Firstly, these large-scaled T2I models exhibit a deficiency in interpretive nuance. This inadequacy is particularly evident in the realm of intricate and culturally enriched art forms, such as Chinese arts. Secondly, a significant portion of editing techniques (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2201-09865, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2304-03246,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2311-17957,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2312-14091, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2212-00932, DBLP:conf/iclr/MengHSSWZE22, DBLP:conf/cvpr/AvrahamiHGGTPLF23}) mainly focuses on modifying content structures, involving actions such as object removal or alteration of specific image segments. Most of these methods are struggle in preserving the detail and accuracy of the original input image. (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2212-06013,DBLP:conf/eccv/LiuLDTT22,DBLP:conf/iclr/HertzMTAPC23}). Lastly, a large portion of stylization methods in diffusion models require extra example-based training (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/MokadyHAPC23,DBLP:journals/tog/ValevskiKMSML23}), which limits its generalization ability. Artistic style transfer is a technique that applies desired artistic styles to content images, creating new artistic images. There are two main types of methods: neural style transfer and pre-trained large-scale model-based methods. Neural style transfer train a feed-forward network to learn style representation from art datasets. While they can preserve the content structure, they often fail to capture deep style features and can introduce unwanted patterns and artifacts. On the other hand, pre-trained large-scale models, using the knowledge from T2I models, can produce more realistic images. Recently, some methods use text prompts or example style images to guide the creation of stylized images. While these methods can generate more realistic results, they often have difficulties preserving the structure of the original content. Additionally, these pre-trained large-scale models often require training on a specific set of artworks, which limits their use in industry. We present Art Creator, a novel method for stylizing images through the prompt of the desired style or example images. This approach adeptly preserves semantic details while modifying the visual aspects of the input, such as colors, brush strokes, and textures. Our method includes two parts: fine-tuning and sampling. For the fine-tuning part, The diffusion-based T2I model underwent a fine-tuning process using both the WikiArt (\cite{wikiart}) dataset and our proposed ChinArt dataset. This finetuned model exhibits an enhanced capacity to comprehend intricate style descriptions, such as art genre and artist information. It is noteworthy that Art Creator distinguishes itself through its singular ability to generate diverse eastern art style paintings, including but not limited to Dunhuang frescos, ink and wash painting, and claborate-style painting. The process of sampling involves two modules. The first module is Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC). This is achieved by incorporating an style condition in the latent space of the Diffusion model. ACC is proficiency in selectively preserving specific details, thereby facilitating a nuanced transfer of both stylistic and geometric attributes. The second module is Texture Enhance Module (TEM). Using a contrastive loss function between the generated sample and the edited prompt, TEM enhances images with detailed textures in a zero-shot manner. Our primary contributions can be summarized as follows: \begin{itemize} \item Proposal of \emph{Art Creator}, an efficient zero-shot stylization method for Text-to-Image (T2I) models. \item Proposal of Adaptive Conditional Control to preserve the content structure and ensure reasonable gradient directions. \item Introduction of the \emph{ChinArt} dataset, comprising over 20,000 labeled eastern artworks. \item We showcased the superior performance of our approach in achieving remarkable performance in content preservation and style transfer compared to other state-of-the-art algorithms. \end{itemize} Our code and data are accessible on \url{https://github.com/tang-shan/Art-Creator}. \begin{figure*}[!htbp] \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2.pdf}} \caption{Examples of ChinArt Dataset} \label{fig} \end{figure*} \section{Related Work} \subsection{Text-to-Image Diffusion} While Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/IsolaZZE17,DBLP:conf/iccv/ZhuPIE17,DBLP:conf/cvpr/KarrasLA19,DBLP:conf/cvpr/KarrasLAHLA20,DBLP:conf/cvpr/ChoiUYH20,DBLP:journals/tog/SongLLMLZC21}) and Autoregressive (AR) models (\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/BrownMRSKDNSSAA20,radford2019language,radford2018improving}) have shown text-to-image (T2I) capabilities in the past years, their image generation often focuses on specific domains rather than diverse open-world sets. In contrast, diffusion models have recently demonstrated its power in generating varied high-quality images. For instance, GLIDE (\cite{nichol2021glide}) has introduced a cascaded diffusion structure for image generation and editing. DALL-E2 (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2204-06125}) proposes a multi-stage model involving CLIP-based (\cite{radford2021learning}) text encoding, image decoding, and upsampling. VQ-Diffusion (\cite{gu2022vector}) explores T2I diffusion models within the discrete latent space of VQ-VAE (\cite{van2017neural}). Stable Diffusion (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/RombachBLEO22}) investigates the diffusion process across the latent space, enhancing both training and sampling efficiency without compromising image quality. These generative models work by adding Gaussian noise in a forward Markov chain process. Then, a trained network is employed to gradually remove the noise and create clean samples. In particular, DDPM (\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/HoJA20}) directly obtains samples $z_t$ from the initial state $z_0$ by introducing Gaussian noise based on $\alpha_t \in (0,1)$ at each time step $t$ within the interval $[1, ..., T]$. \begin{equation} z_t=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_t}z_0+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_t}\epsilon \end{equation} where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0,I)$, $\overline{\alpha}_t = \prod_{i=0}^t \alpha_i$. The DDIM (\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/SongME21}) reverse sampling process is given by: \begin{equation} \begin{split} x_s=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_s}\frac{z_t-\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_t}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)}{\overline{\alpha}_t}\\+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_s-\sigma_t^2}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)+\sigma_t^2 \epsilon \end{split} \end{equation} The neural network $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)$ is employed to predict the noise, serving as a score function with a scaling factor. $\sigma_t$ represents the noise variance, influencing the stochastic of the sampling process. In our study, we set $\sigma_t=0$ to remove randomness from the DDIM sampling procedure. Classifier-free Guidance (CFG) is a technique used to balance the quality and variety of samples produced by a diffusion model. In practice, it involves training the model for both conditional and unconditional denoising.During inference, the two score estimates are combined. By increasing a guidance scale $w \geq 1$, the adjusted score estimate $\overline \epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)$ is extrapolated in the direction towards the conditional score $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)$ and away from the unconditional score $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)$ where $y$ represents the prompt. \begin{equation} \overline \epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)=(w+1)\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)-w\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t) \end{equation} \subsection{Style Transfer} Style transfer involves transforming the style of an image into another one while maintaining its content semantic. In recent years, both GAN-based (Generative Adversarial Network) (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/Wang0ZTKC18,DBLP:conf/icmcs/ZhuangY21,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2005-07702,DBLP:conf/eccv/ChongF22,DBLP:conf/isica/ChenLC19}) and CNN-based (Convolutional Neural Network) methods (\cite{DBLP:conf/iccv/HuangB17,DBLP:conf/nips/LiFYWLY17,DBLP:conf/iccv/LiuLHLWLSLD21,DBLP:conf/cvpr/ParkL19,DBLP:conf/eccv/WuZDB22,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2201-02233}) have been developed. GAN-based approaches are often constrained to a specific domain. For example, CartoonGAN (\cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/ChenLL18}) can only generate cartoon style images and AnimeGAN (\cite{DBLP:conf/isica/ChenLC19}) is restricted to animation style. CNN-based methods have achieved success in transferring color and texture, but it struggles with more intricate style attributes, such as geometry and shape. Recently, the development of large-scale Text-to-Image (T2I) models has led to the creation of many pre-trained large-scale model-based style transfer methods. ArtBank (\cite{DBLP:conf/aaai/ZhangZXLZSLLHL24}) introduced an Implicit Style Prompt Bank (ISPB) to learn and store knowledge from a set of artworks, while LSAST\cite{DBLP:conf/ijcai/ZhangZL0MHXLL0Z24} proposed a Step-aware and Layer-aware Prompt Space to dynamically regulate the content structure and style of input images. Although these pre-trained diffusion based artistic style transfer methods can create highly realistic stylized images, their training is time-consuming. \subsection{Diffusion-based Image Editing Methods} In recent years, the advancement of Text-to-Image (T2I) models has significantly boosted the progress of Diffusion-based image editing methods (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2204-06125,DBLP:conf/iclr/MengHSSWZE22}). These techniques involve modifying the sampling process of a pre-trained T2I generator, requiring minimal or no additional training of the networks. For instance, the Prompt-to-Prompt (P2P) method introduced by \cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/HertzMTAPC23} edits images through cross-attention control. Another approach, Composable Diffusion (CompDiff), proposed by \cite{DBLP:conf/eccv/LiuLDTT22}, focuses on compositional visual generation using diffusion models. Additionally, \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2212-06013} presented Stable Artist, a method that offers artistic control by guiding the diffusion process in different semantic directions. Stylization is regarded as one type of edition. Nevertheless, these image editing techniques often lack precise control over specific semantic regions and struggle to transfer detailed textures and brush strokes. Furthermore, they do not facilitate image-to-image translation. \begin{figure*}[!htbp] \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig3.pdf}} \caption{An overview of the proposed score distillation based fine-tune method} \label{fig} \end{figure*} \begin{table} \begin{center} \caption{Comparison with WikiArt Dataset} \label{tab1} \begin{tabular}{p{5.2cm} r{1.5cm} r{1.5cm}} \hline & WikiArt & ChinArt \\ \hline Number of Chinese Artists & 51 & 112\\ \hline Number of Labeled Chinese Artworks & 1,437 & 21,762 \\ \hline Labelled by Painting Tools & No & Yes \\ \hline \end{tabular} \end{center} \end{table} \section{Method} The framework of our method is presented in Figure 1. It includes three parts: Score Distillation based Fine-tune (SDFT), Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC) and Texture Enhance Module (TEM). First, the diffusion model is fine-tuned using labeled art datasets, including the WikiArt (\cite{wikiart}) dataset and our proposed ChinArt dataset. Then, stylization is applied to images generated by the refined model through a diffusion reverse sampling process. Let $I$ denote an image generated by the fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model with the text prompt $y_c$. The object of stylization is editing the input image by the style condition $y_s$, resulting in a stylized image $I^*$.The sampling process encompasses two key modules: Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC), which adjusts the overall appearance based on the style condition, and Texture Enhancement Module (TEM) adds texture details to the generated image. Both steps can be done in a single sampling process. \subsection{ChinArt Dataset} The ChinArt dataset contains a total of 43,077 images, some of them are manually selected from image websites, some of them are collected from offline exhibitions, others are obtained through Google and Baidu searches to enhance dataset diversity. Each image undergoes manual labeling, including details such as art genre, artist name, and painting tools, etc. The dataset is categorized into two classes. \begin{figure}[!htbp] \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig4.pdf}} \caption{The visualization of how $K$ varies in ACC module} \label{fig} \end{figure} \begin{itemize} \item Western Painting (5 types, 17,811 in total): sketch, oil painting, watercolor painting, color pencil painting and pen drawing. \item Eastern Painting (7 types, 25,266 in total): claborate-style painting, ink and wash painting, Dunhuang frescoes, line drawing, Thangka, traditional new year pictures and Ukiyoe. \end{itemize} In comparison to the WikiArt dataset, the ChinArt dataset encompasses a larger collection of Chinese artworks, including pieces representing a broader range of traditional Chinese painting styles. Examples of ChinArt dataset is shown in Figure 2. \subsection{Score Distillation based Fine-tune} Some approaches have been used for fine-tuning the diffusion model, including hypernetworks (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/HaDL16}), LoRA (\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/HuSWALWWC22}), and LyCORIS (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2309-14859}). These techniques are commonly employed to generate a specific style. After training the model with only a few reference images (often from a few to hundreds), it can produce images with similar stylistic characteristics. However, these methods pose challenges for our fine-tuning task, primarily due to the extensive size of our dataset, exceeding 110,000 images, making it hard to effectively learn with a limited number of parameters. An alternative approach involves training the entire U-Net network, but this method meets difficulties in avoiding catastrophic forgetting as the training epoch progresses. The introduction of new data leads to a degradation in performance on previously learned patterns. We present a new fine-tuning method called Score Distillation based Fine-tune (SDFT) (\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/PooleJBM23}). Figure 3 shows an overview of this approach. The training data for SDFT comes from two sources: the Laion Dataset (\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/SchuhmannBVGWCC22}) and the Art Dataset (WikiArt and ChinArt). The Laion-5B dataset has been used to train large T2I models like Stable Diffusion and Imagen. Our method uses alternating training techniques to balance learning new information while preserving what the model has already learned. During the art data training steps, art images are passed through a pre-trained image caption model (BLIP2) to get a description of the content, then combined with the style description provided by the art dataset label to generate the final prompt for the artwork. This image-text pair is used to train the diffusion model. For the Laion data training steps, we use a pre-trained diffusion model as a teacher to guide the learning process. The teacher model is frozen. The goal is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (\cite{1951Leibler}) between scores predicted by the teacher model and the student model, helping to prevent catastrophic forgetting. In practice, we performed $N$ rounds of Laion data training followed by one round of Art data training. Section 6.2.1 provides detailed pseudocode for SDFT. The loss function is given by: \begin{equation} L_{SD}=KL(p_{\theta_1}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)||p_{\theta_2}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)) \end{equation} In the equation, $p_{\theta_1}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)$ is the conditional probability density of the teacher model, and $p_{\theta_2}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)$ represents the conditional probability density of the student model. In Section 6.1.1, we demonstrate that this involves the gradient derivation of the probability density, which corresponds to the learned score functions from the diffusion model. \begin{equation} L_{SD}=\frac{(1-\alpha_t)^2}{\alpha_t(1-\overline \alpha_t)}||\epsilon_{\theta_1}(z_t,t,y)-\epsilon_{\theta_2}(z_t,t,y)||_2^2 \end{equation} \begin{figure*}[!h] \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig5.pdf}} \caption{Art Creator enables artistic creation through fine-tuning and zero-shot sampling. It allows users to incorporate various artistic styles without the need for retraining. Users can control style through many flexible ways, such as painting tool ('Crayon', 'Pen' and 'drawing'), artist name ('Monet','Picasso' and 'Kandinsky') and art style ('Cubism','Ukiyoe','Pop art').} \label{fig} \end{figure*} \subsection{Adaptive Conditional Control} The core concept behind Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC) is to impact the diffusion process by employing two prompts: one for content and another for style. To delve into ACC, we'll begin by considering a single direction in classifier-free guidance. \begin{equation} p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y) \propto p(z_{t-1}|z_t)p(y|z_t)^w \end{equation} The distribution $p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y)$ is the distribution of $z_{t-1}$ conditioned on $y$, $p(z_{t-1}|z_t)$ is unconditional distribution. $p(y|z_t)$ is the class distribution. $w$ is the guidance scale. The noise predicted by the classifier-free guidance in one direction is: \begin{equation} \overline \epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)=(w+1)\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)-w\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t) \end{equation} $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)$ is the unconditioned score estimate and $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)$ is the the conditioned estimate. We are now prepared to progress from employing a single direction to considering two concepts, specifically content and style control. \begin{equation} p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s) \propto p(z_{t-1}|z_t)p(y_c|z_{t})^\alpha p(y_s|z_t)^\beta \end{equation} where $y_c$ is the content condition and $y_s$ is the style condition. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are guidance scales for content and style control. The noise predicted by the classifier-free guidance in this two direction is: \begin{equation} \epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c,y_s)=(1-\alpha-\beta)\epsilon_\theta(z_t)+\alpha\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c) +\beta\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_s) \end{equation} Some diffusion-based image editing approaches, such as DreamStyler and Stable Artist, rely on above multiple condition guidance. (\cite{DBLP:conf/eccv/LiuLDTT22,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2212-06013,DBLP:conf/cvpr/BrooksHE23,DBLP:conf/aaai/AhnLLKKNH24})}. However, we find that maintaining content structure proves challenging with this simplistic multiple-directional control. In practice, $p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s)$ is the product of three elements:$p(z_{t-1}|z_t)$,$p(y_c|z_{t})$ and $p(y_s|z_{t})$. The scale factors $\alpha$ and $\beta$ enhance the sharpness of the distributions. The distribution $p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s)$ becomes poorly conditioned when any of the three distributions in its low-density regions. This scenario is likely when there is a significant disparity between the content and style prompts or when textual inversion methods (\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/GalAAPBCC23}) are utilized to obtain the style prompt. To preserve the content structure and ensure a reasonable gradient direction, our strategy involves guiding the diffusion model through Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC). \begin{equation} p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s) \propto p(z_{t-1}|z_t)p(y_c|z_{t})^\alpha p(y_s|z_t)^{\beta K(z_t,y_c,y_s)} \end{equation} $K(z_t,y_c,y_s)$ is the adaptive factor. \begin{equation} K(z_t,y_c,y_s)=\frac{p(y_s|z_t)}{p(y_c|z_t)+p(y_s|z_t)} \end{equation} The noise predicted by our method is: \begin{equation} \begin{split} \epsilon_\theta(z_t,y_c,y_s)=\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)+\alpha(\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c)-\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t))\\ +\beta K(z_t,y_c,y_s)(\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_s)-\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)) \end{split} \end{equation} where: \begin{gather} K(z_t,y_c,y_s)=\frac{u_2}{u_1+u_2}\\ u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c)\\ u_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_s) \end{gather} We provide detailed derivation in Section 6.1.2. To better understand how $K$ changes at different denoising timesteps, we conduct a toy experiment using 50 steps for DDIM sampling. We used ChatGPT to randomly generate 100 style descriptions and 100 content descriptions, pairing them together to create the descriptions for generating images. During the image generation process, we recorded the changes of self-adaptive factor $k$. Figure 4 shows the average variation trend of $K$. As shown in Figure 4, we observe that $K$ varies significantly during the middle timesteps (from 15 to 35). We hypothesize that this is because the diffusion model generates images from low to high-frequency details, with the middle stages producing more structured features. The gradient difference between the content and style prompts is largest during this period. This finding aligns with the conclusion in \cite{DBLP:journals/tog/ZhangDTHHMLDX23}. \subsection{Texture Enhance Module} The output produced by ACC shows the coarse style characteristics of the style prompt but lacks finer details such as brush strokes. To tackle this limitation, we introduce a Texture Enhancement Module (TEM). This module guides the diffusion model towards enhancing texture by computing patchwise losses using the CLIP model (\cite{radford2021learning}) and incorporating gradients into the denoised image at each time step. \begin{figure}[!htbp] \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig6.pdf}} \caption{Qualitative Results with Different Training Strategy} \label{fig} \end{figure} The patchwise loss function is mainly motivated by \cite{DBLP:conf/cvpr/KwonY22}. Let $I_{adc}$ represent the image stylized by adaptive control. $vae(.)$ denotes the VAE encoder (\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/RazaviOV19,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2104-10157}), and $vae^{-1}(.)$ denotes its inverse operation.$E_I$ and $E_T$ are the image and text encoders of CLIP, respectively. $aug(.)$ signifies random geometrical augmentations such as cropping, flipping, and perspective transformations. $\tau$ denotes the threshold value, and $N$ represents the number of patches. The patchwise loss function is defined as follows: \begin{equation} \Delta T=E_T(y_s)-E_T(y_c) \end{equation} \begin{equation} \Delta I=E_I(aug(vae^{-1}\frac{(z_t-\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_t}\epsilon_{\theta}(z_t,t,y_c))}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_t}}))-E_I(I_{adc}) \end{equation} \begin{equation} l_{patch}^i=1-\frac{\Delta I \Delta T}{|\Delta I||\Delta T|} \end{equation} \begin{equation} L_{patch}=\frac{1}{N}max(l_{patch}^i,\tau) \end{equation} The ACC module and the TEM module work together during the diffusion model's sampling process. The score function of denoising process is the weighted sum of $\epsilon_\theta(z_t,y_c,y_s)$ and the gradient of the loss patch. \begin{equation} \begin{split} \epsilon(z_t,y_c,y_s)=\epsilon_\theta(z_t,y_c,y_s)+\lambda \frac{\partial L_{patch}}{\partial z_t} \end{split} \end{equation} where $\lambda$ is a hyper-parameter to control the strength of TEM. We incorporate the DDIM strategy for the reverse sampling process. The denoised image $z_s$ is predicted by the function below. \begin{equation} \begin{split} \hat z_s=\sqrt {\overline \alpha_s}\frac{z_t-\sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}\epsilon(z_t,y_c,y_s)}{\sqrt {\overline \alpha_t}} \\+\sqrt{1-\overline \alpha_s}\epsilon(z_t,y_c,y_s)+\lambda \frac{\partial L_{patch}}{\partial z_t} \end{split} \end{equation} In image-to-image scenarios, we employ the Gram matrix (\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/GatysEB15a}) as a substitute for the patchwise loss function. \begin{table} \begin{center} \caption{Quantitative Results with Different Training Strategy} \label{tab1e} \begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm} r{1.7cm} r{1.7cm}} \hline & Clip Score & Aesthetic Score \\ \hline Stable Diffusion & 32.5 & 5.17\\ \hline U-Net & 28.67 & 5.14 \\ \hline LoRA & 29.29 & 5.18 \\ \hline SDFT & \textbf{32.68} & \textbf{5.24} \\ \hline \end{tabular} \end{center} \end{table} \section{Result} \subsection{Experiment Setting} So far, the most state-of-the-art Stable Diffusion is 3.5. It is similar to distillation, but uses score functions instead of densities. This loss function helps retain the knowledge of pre-trained diffusion model. This section outlines the derivation of Equation 5, starting with an analysis of the KL term. \begin{equation} L_{SD}=KL(p_{\theta_1}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y)||p_{\theta_2}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y)) \end{equation} where $p_{\theta_1}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y) \sim \mathcal N(u_1,\sigma^2I)$ and $p_{\theta_2}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y) \sim \mathcal N(u_2,\sigma^2I)$. The KL divergence of two n-dimensional normal distributions ($p_1 \sim \mathcal N(u_1,\Sigma_1)$,$p_2 \sim \mathcal N(u_2,\Sigma_2)$) can be calculated by the formula below: \begin{equation} \begin{split} KL(p_1||p_2)=\frac{1}{2}(log\frac{|\Sigma_2|}{|\Sigma_1|}-n+ tr(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1)\\+(u_2-u_1)^T\Sigma_2^{-1}(u_2-u_1)) \end{split} \end{equation} The KL divergence of $p_{\theta_1}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y)$ and $p_{\theta_2}(z_{t-1}|z_t,y)$ can be denoted as: \begin{equation} \begin{split} KL(p_{\theta_1}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)||p_{\theta_2}(z_t|z_{t-1},y))\\ =\frac{1}{2}(-n+n+\frac{1}{\sigma^2}(u_2-u_1)^T(u_2-u_1))\\ =\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(u_2-u_1)^T(u_2-u_1) \end{split} \end{equation} In diffusion models, the mean parameters $u_1$ and $u_2$ correspond to the score function. \begin{equation} \begin{split} u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y)\\ u_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y) \end{split} \end{equation} Putting these together, KL divergence between teacher model and student model is: \begin{equation} \begin{split} KL(p_{\theta_1}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)||p_{\theta_2}(z_t|z_{t-1},y)) =\frac{(1-\alpha_t)^2}{\alpha_t(1-\overline \alpha_t)}\\||\epsilon_{\theta_1}(z_t,t,y)-\epsilon_{\theta_2}(z_t,t,y)||_2^2 \end{split} \end{equation} \subsubsection{Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC)} The motivation behind Adaptive Conditional Control (ACC) is inspired by classifier-free guidance (CFG). It is a method to balance diversity and fidelity in conditional diffusion models. \begin{equation} p(z_t|z_{t-1},y) \propto p(z_t|z_{t-1})p(y|z_{t})^w \end{equation} $p(z_t|z_{t-1})$ is the unconditional probability and $p(y|z_{t})$ is the classifier probability. $w$ is the scale facotr. This single-conditional CFG can be extended to multiple conditions by providing multiple condtions. \begin{equation} \begin{split} \bigtriangledown_{z_t} log p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s) \\ = \bigtriangledown_{z_t} logp(z_{t-1}|z_t) +\alpha \bigtriangledown_{z_t}logp(y_c|z_{t})\\ + \beta\bigtriangledown_{z_t} logp(y_s|z_t)\\ =(1-\alpha-\beta)\bigtriangledown_{z_t}logp(z_{t-1}|z_t)\\+\alpha\bigtriangledown_{z_t}logp(z_t|y_c)+\beta\bigtriangledown_{z_t}logp(z_t|y_s) \end{split} \end{equation} The noise predicted with two conditions is: \begin{equation} \begin{split} \epsilon(z_t,t,y_c,y_s)=(1-\alpha-\beta)\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t)+\\ \alpha\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c) +\beta\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_s) \end{split} \end{equation} The adaptive control is based on two-conditional CFG, but adding a self-adaptive factor to avoid wrong score prediction. \begin{equation} p(z_{t-1}|z_t,y_c,y_s) \propto p(z_{t-1}|z_t)p(y_c|z_{t})^\alpha p(y_s|z_t)^{\beta K(z_t,y_s,y_c)} \end{equation} $K(z_t,y_s,y_c)$ is the adaptive factor. Now we deduce the Eqn. 13. \begin{equation} \begin{split} K(z_t,y_s,y_c)=\mathbb{E}_{z_t,y_c,y_s}[\frac{p(y_s|z_t)}{p(y_c|z_t)+p(y_s|z_t)}]\\ =\mathbb{E}_{z_t,y_c,y_s}[(\frac{p(y_s)p(z_t|y_s)}{p(y_c)p(z_t|y_c)+p(y_s)p(z_t|y_s)}] \end{split} \end{equation} The probabilities $p(y_c)$ and $p(y_s)$ represent the likelihood of occurrence of the content prompt and style prompt, respectively. One method to compute these probabilities is by calculating the occurrence rate of words in the training set, but this approach can be highly time-consuming. Additionally, high-frequency words may dominate the probability distribution, resulting in near-zero probabilities for other words. Hence, we simplify the computation by counting number of words in the prompt, which has been found to yield satisfactory results. Because $p(z_t|y_s) \sim \mathcal N(u_1,\sigma^2 I)$,$p(y_c)p(z_t|y_c)+p(y_s)p(z_t|y_s) \sim \mathcal N(p(y_c)u_1+p(y_s)u_2,(p(y_c)^2+p(y_s)^2)\sigma^2 I)$. The division of two normal distributions with different variation is not a distribution density and this expectation is intractable. We take a approximation. \begin{equation} K(z_t,y_s,y_c) \approx \frac{\mathbb{E}_{z_t,y_s}[p(y_s)p(z_t|y_s)]}{\mathbb{E}_{z_t,y_c,y_s}[p(y_c)p(z_t|y_c)+p(y_s)p(z_t|y_s)]} \end{equation} The result is: \begin{equation} K(z_t,y_s,y_c) \approx \frac{p(y_s)u_2}{p(y_c)u_1+p(y_s)u_2} \end{equation} where \begin{gather} u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_c)\\ u_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt \alpha_t}z_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt \alpha_t \sqrt {1-\overline \alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(z_t,t,y_s) \end{gather}
